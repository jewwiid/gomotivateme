# Direct-response ad formats

Creative *wrappers* used in Meta, TikTok, and Reels ads. They are not placements (Feed vs Stories) and they are not offers. The same product can be sold in any of these skins.

Compiled from two public lists: `_tomlewis` “42 best-converting ad formats” and `ignelmes` “40 ad formats worth every penny.” Duplicate names are merged. Close cousins (script vs screenshot, doodle vs doctor) stay separate because they look different in the feed.

**Expected result** here means what the format is *built to do* in the auction: stop the thumb, earn a second of trust, or push a click. It is not a promise that the ad will convert.

## 1. AI podcast

**What it means.** Two AI voices (or a fake “podcast clip”) discuss the product as if it came up on a show. Often a split waveform, fake show name, and a “wait, that actually works?” beat.

**Expected result.** Entertainment plus borrowed authority. Viewers treat it like overheard conversation, not a sales page, so they stay longer. Weak if it sounds like two robots reading a script.

## 2. Breaking news

**What it means.** News-ticker, “BREAKING,” red lower-third, or a fake headline card. The “story” is a launch, a restock, a study, or a competitor getting called out.

**Expected result.** Novelty bias. People stop because news layout means “this just happened.” Works for launches. Burns trust if the news is fake.

## 3. Bundle offer

**What it means.** Several SKUs or bonuses stacked as one deal: “Get A + B + C for one price.” Often a table of “value” vs “today’s price.”

**Expected result.** Higher average order value and a reason to buy *this* package instead of thinking. Needs a real bundle. Fake value math gets ignored.

## 4. Case of emergency

**What it means.** Siren language: “If this is you, stop scrolling.” Medical, financial, or social emergency framing around a problem the product claims to handle.

**Expected result.** Fear of delay. High thumb-stop, high complaint risk. Converts anxious buyers; trains others to mute you.

## 5. Claymation

**What it means.** Stop-motion clay, Play-Doh, or toy figures acting out the problem and the product.

**Expected result.** Pattern interrupt. Almost nothing else in the ad auction looks like this, so hold rate can jump. Production is slow. The story still has to sell.

## 6. Crossed-out problems

**What it means.** A list of pains. Each line gets a strikethrough as the product “removes” it. Last line sometimes stays uncrossed as the honest leftover.

**Expected result.** Visual completion. The viewer watches problems disappear and maps themselves onto the list. Cheap to make. Collapses if you strike through things the product does not actually fix.

## 7. Customer testimonial

**What it means.** A real buyer on camera, in a voice note, or quoted with a name and result. Not the founder. Not a stock actor if you can help it.

**Expected result.** Trust transfer. One specific human outcome beats ten feature bullets. Dead without real customers, or if the quote is generic (“game changer”).

## 8. Don't be an idiot

**What it means.** Insult-as-hook. “Only an idiot still does X.” The product is framed as the obvious intelligent choice.

**Expected result.** Status and shame. Polarises: some people click to prove they are not the idiot; others bounce. High engagement, brand-damage risk.

## 9. Don't buy this

**What it means.** Reverse pitch. “Don’t buy this if you want Y. Buy it if you want Z.” Or “don’t buy the expensive version.” Often used to knock a category, not the seller’s own product.

**Expected result.** Curiosity plus qualification. Filters tire-kickers and makes the remaining pitch feel honest. Fails when it is only a gimmick and the ad still begs you to buy.

## 10. Doodle

**What it means.** Hand-drawn stick figures, arrows, and circles on a blank page or tablet. The founder (or a hand) explains the idea while drawing.

**Expected result.** Clarity. Drawing slows the explanation down so the mechanism can land. Cheap, human, easy to remake. Low polish can help or look amateur depending on the category.

## 11. Doctor

**What it means.** Lab coat, clinic backdrop, “as a [profession] I recommend.” Sometimes a real clinician, often a lookalike.

**Expected result.** Authority bias. Strong in health, skin, sleep, supplements. Legal and platform risk if it implies medical claims you cannot back. Viewers are trained to distrust fake doctors.

## 12. Email screenshot

**What it means.** A cropped Gmail/Outlook thread: a customer asking a question, a “we messed up” note, a waitlist ping, a founder reply.

**Expected result.** Voyeurism. It feels like you were not supposed to see it, so it reads as real. Works for proof, support quality, or a quiet offer. Fake-looking UI kits kill it.

## 13. Google search

**What it means.** A search bar types a question the customer actually Googles. Results, an AI overview, or a “People also ask” block appear, then the product is the answer.

**Expected result.** Intent match. The viewer thinks “that is my question.” High relevance, low production. Overused; the query has to be specific.

## 14. Greenscreen

**What it means.** Creator filmed on green screen, reacting in front of a review, a Reddit thread, a landing page, or a competitor ad.

**Expected result.** Dual attention: face for emotion, background for proof. TikTok-native. The comment track has to add something the screenshot does not already say.

## 15. Hack 101

**What it means.** Tutorial packaging: “The 30-second hack,” “101,” numbered steps, lo-fi screen recording.

**Expected result.** Save/share behaviour. People keep “useful” ads. Converts when the hack is the product in use, not a bait tip that never mentions what you sell.

## 16. Instagram Story

**What it means.** The ad is dressed as an IG Story: progress bar, poll sticker, “swipe up” / link sticker, music chip, messy selfie framing.

**Expected result.** Native camouflage. Stories-trained thumbs do not skip as fast. Best as actual Stories placements; as a Feed video it can look like a cheap fake.

## 17. iPhone Notes

**What it means.** The yellow Notes app (or a close clone). A short typed confession, list, or late-night thought. Often one typo left in.

**Expected result.** Intimacy. It feels like a private thought, not creative. Fast to produce, easy to test headlines. Dies if the note is obviously ad copy in a notes skin.

## 18. Long form

**What it means.** 60–180+ seconds. Mechanism, story, objection handling, then offer. Talking head, documentary, or slideshow with VO.

**Expected result.** Warmth and conversion on people who stay. Higher hold requirement; losers waste spend. Use when the product needs explanation, not when the only message is “20% off.”

## 19. Low-stock alert

**What it means.** Inventory UI, “3 left,” restock timer, sold-out crossed with “back tonight.”

**Expected result.** Scarcity. Speeds up people who already wanted it. Trains disbelief if stock is always “almost gone.” Useless if you cannot or will not sell out.

## 20. Meme

**What it means.** An existing meme template with product text dropped in. Reaction images, Drake format, expanding brain, etc.

**Expected result.** Cheap reach and comments. Rarely sells on its own. Best as a top-of-funnel interrupt, then a tighter ad for clickers. Copyright and “trying too hard” are the failure modes.

## 21. Myth vs fact

**What it means.** Two columns or a flip: “Myth: … / Fact: …” The myth is the belief that stops the purchase; the fact is your reframe.

**Expected result.** Correction. People pause to see if they were wrong. High save rate when the fact is actually new. Falls apart if the “myth” is a straw man.

## 22. Native

**What it means.** Looks like a normal post from that platform: a tweet, a casual selfie, a UGC clip with no logo in the first seconds, a photo dump.

**Expected result.** Lower ad-blindness. CTR from people who would skip a branded bumper. Needs to still contain a reason to click, or you buy cheap views and no action.

## 23. New vs old

**What it means.** Split screen or swipe: old method / old product / old you vs the new version.

**Expected result.** Contrast. The brain likes binaries. Works for version-2 launches, “stop doing it the hard way,” and category swaps. Weak if both sides look the same.

## 24. Offer

**What it means.** The ad *is* the deal. Price, discount, deadline, code, “shop now.” Little story.

**Expected result.** Direct conversion from people already in-market. Efficient on retargeting. Cold audiences bounce; there is nothing to care about yet.

## 25. Problem vs solution

**What it means.** Left/right or sequential: life with the problem, life with the product. More literal than transformation; often objects or workflows, not bodies.

**Expected result.** Instant comprehension. The viewer should get the job of the product in two seconds. If the “solution” still needs a paragraph, pick long form.

## 26. Problems

**What it means.** A stacked list of pains with no strikethrough and no solution until the end (or in the caption). Rapid cuts, overlay text.

**Expected result.** Recognition. “That’s me” is the whole first beat. Must land on a product action quickly or it is just a vent.

## 27. Reasons why

**What it means.** “7 reasons this works” / “X reasons to switch.” Carousel or fast text-on-screen list. Last item is usually the offer.

**Expected result.** Completeness. Lists get watched to the end. Good for objection handling. Reason 1 must be strong or they never reach 7.

## 28. Reddit style

**What it means.** Fake or real Reddit UI: AITA, r/BuyItForLife, upvotes, a thread that concludes the product is the answer. Greenscreen-on-Reddit is the video version.

**Expected result.** Peer proof. Reddit still reads as “regular people,” not ads. Using a real thread (with permission / fair use care) beats a mocked-up one. Mock-ups that get the UI wrong feel like scams.

## 29. Screenshot

**What it means.** Phone or desktop capture: checkout, app UI, Stripe payout, DMs, analytics, a 5-star review in context.

**Expected result.** Evidence. Harder to fake than a lifestyle shot (until it is faked). Best when the screenshot *is* the product doing the job.

## 30. Script

**What it means.** A written talking-head script, usually with big captions. Hook line, problem, unique mechanism, proof, CTA. The format people mean by “UGC script.”

**Expected result.** Repeatable testing. You can swap hooks without reshooting the whole world. Performance tracks the first line more than the footage quality.

## 31. Side effect

**What it means.** “The weird side effect of using X is Y.” Y is a benefit you did not lead with (sleep, compliments, time, money).

**Expected result.** Curiosity. The “side effect” frame makes a benefit feel discovered, not claimed. Dangerous in health ads if it sounds like an undisclosed drug effect.

## 32. Stat headline

**What it means.** One number owns the frame: “76%,” “3 hours a week,” “$0.” Source in small type if you have one.

**Expected result.** Fast credibility. Numbers stop skimmers. Invented stats destroy you when someone checks. Small true numbers beat large fake ones.

## 33. Text message

**What it means.** iMessage/WhatsApp thread. Friend, partner, or customer texts about the problem; someone drops a link or a photo of the product.

**Expected result.** Social proof inside a scene people already live in. High relatability. Fake blue-bubble ads are everywhere; specific names and messy timing help.

## 34. Text on skin

**What it means.** Claims written on an arm, stomach, or face with marker, then revealed on camera.

**Expected result.** Pattern interrupt plus body-as-billboard. Common in fitness and skin. Can feel gimmicky or invasive outside those categories.

## 35. Tier list

**What it means.** S / A / B / C / D ranking. Methods, products, ingredients, habits. Your product lands in S. Competitors or old methods land lower.

**Expected result.** Opinion content people argue with, which the algorithm likes. Positions you as the editor. Cheap if you attack rivals; you also invite comparison shopping.

## 36. Transformation

**What it means.** Classic before → after. Body, room, revenue screenshot, skill, skin, a calendar of streaks.

**Expected result.** Desire. The after-state is the product. Highest converting format in categories with visible change. Needs real, dated, comparable photos. Banned or limited in some verticals when it overclaims.

## 37. Trustpilot reviews

**What it means.** Screen recording of Trustpilot, Google, or Amazon reviews. Cursor scrolls, a 4.8 is circled, a long review is read aloud.

**Expected result.** Third-party trust. Stronger than a testimonial you could have written. You need volume and recency. Cherry-picking only 5-stars while hiding 2-stars backfires if they click through.

## 38. Us vs them

**What it means.** Two tribes. “People who still do X” vs “people who switched.” Competitor roast, old-school vs new-school, cheap vs premium.

**Expected result.** Identity. Buyers join a side. Great for differentiation. Ugly when it is only competitor-bashing or punches down at the customer’s current life.

## 39. Us vs us

**What it means.** Internal split: disciplined you vs tired you, 8pm you vs 8am you, the version that wants the goal vs the version that hides.

**Expected result.** Self-recognition without an external enemy. Often softer than us vs them and more shareable. Can slide into grind-shame if the “losing” self is mocked.

## 40. Venn diagram

**What it means.** Two or three circles. The joke or the pitch lives in the overlap (“things that make you happy” ∩ “things you can buy”).

**Expected result.** A beat of intelligence. People pause to read the labels. Easy static image. The overlap has to be a real insight, not a logo parody with no point.

## 41. Warning

**What it means.** Red banner: “WARNING,” “only 24 hours,” “do not use if.” Looks like a label, a recall, or a legal disclaimer.

**Expected result.** Fear and scarcity in one frame. Very high stop rate on mobile. Compliance risk. Audience learns to ignore you if every ad is a warning.

## 42. We're sorry

**What it means.** Apology from the brand: we were out of stock, we underpriced it, we made you wait, we were wrong about Y.

**Expected result.** Humanity plus a reason the offer exists *today*. Works after a real mess or a real restock. Fake apologies for “being too popular” feel like a script.

## 43. Whiteboard

**What it means.** Marker on a whiteboard or glass. Funnel, mechanism, math, “why it works.” Founder in frame optional.

**Expected result.** Competence. Teaching is a trust proxy. Common in B2B, finance, info products. Lighting and handwriting have to be readable on a phone.

## 44. X signs

**What it means.** “5 signs you need this.” Checklist of symptoms. If you nod at three, the CTA is for you.

**Expected result.** Self-selection. The ad qualifies the viewer. Strong when signs are specific. “Sign 1: you have a phone” is not a sign.

## 45. You can avoid

**What it means.** “You can avoid X, Y, Z.” X is the expensive, embarrassing, or slow path. The product is the avoid-path.

**Expected result.** Loss aversion. Avoiding pain often beats chasing a gain. Keep X true. Invented catastrophes make the whole ad feel like a warning-label cousin.

## 46. Zero stars

**What it means.** A 0.0 or 1-star review of the *old way*, the competitor, or “doing nothing.” Sometimes a fake 1-star of your product that you then rebut (“they were mad it actually worked”).

**Expected result.** Pattern interrupt (reviews are supposed to be positive) plus humour. The rebuttal version can work as a testimonial in disguise. Risky if it looks like you are fabricating reviews.

## How to use the list

Pick the **job** first, then the skin.

| If you need… | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| A stop in a crowded feed | Claymation, text on skin, meme, warning, zero stars, Venn |
| Trust on cold traffic | Testimonial, Trustpilot, screenshot, Reddit, email screenshot |
| A clear explanation | Whiteboard, doodle, long form, script, problem vs solution |
| A click from people who already want it | Offer, bundle, low-stock, breaking news |
| Identity | Us vs them, us vs us, tier list, don’t buy this |
| Recognition (“that’s me”) | iPhone Notes, text message, X signs, problems, crossed-out problems |

Most “winning ads” are a **hook format** (Notes, warning, Venn) attached to a **proof format** (screenshot, testimonial) attached to an **offer**. Testing the wrapper without changing the product story is the whole point of this list.
