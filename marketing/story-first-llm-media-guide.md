# Story-first media guide for LLMs

Use this guide whenever an LLM writes public-facing content for GoMotivateMe: social posts, captions, launch copy, video scripts, email introductions, founder posts, web copy, or talking points.

The source principle is simple: **story earns attention, but the story must be about the person we serve.** GoMotivateMe is not the hero asking for an audience. The person pursuing a meaningful goal is the hero. We are the experienced, warm guide who helps them move forward with public accountability and support.

This guide adapts the supplied business-storytelling transcript into operational copy rules. It is a writing framework, not permission to make unsupported performance, psychological, or market claims.

## The narrative roles

| Role | GoMotivateMe expression |
| --- | --- |
| Hero | A person trying to make progress on a goal that matters to them. |
| Problem / "hole" | Isolation, inertia, a loss of momentum, or having nobody to share progress with. Do not exaggerate hardship. |
| Guide | GoMotivateMe: empathetic because goals are hard to sustain alone; competent because it provides a simple public goal page, progress updates, and supportive reactions. |
| Plan | Create a goal, share one link with chosen supporters, post progress, receive encouragement, keep going. |
| Transformation | The person feels seen and supported as they make meaningful progress. Do not promise outcomes. |
| Villain | Isolation, silence, and the friction of pursuing a goal alone. Never attack people, competitors, or a user's current habits. |

## Non-negotiable writing rules

1. **Make the audience the hero.** Use `you`, `people pursuing a goal`, or a specific relatable person as the active character. The customer gets to make progress; the brand helps.
2. **Write as a guide, never as a hero.** Do not frame the company, founder, product launch, funding, feature, or internal ambition as the main journey.
3. **Lead with a moment of recognition.** Start with a concrete tension, feeling, scene, or observation the audience can recognise—not a feature list or company announcement.
4. **Show empathy before explaining the product.** Name why the problem is difficult, then introduce the smallest relevant product action.
5. **Keep the story brief and memorable.** One post should normally contain one person, one tension, one helpful action, and one clear next step.
6. **Be entertaining in a human way.** Prefer specificity, contrast, rhythm, honest observation, and a visual moment. Do not use empty hype, forced jokes, rage bait, or manufactured drama.
7. **Make claims that can be supported.** Do not claim neurological effects, sales outcomes, clinical benefits, market leadership, or user results unless the supplied brief includes reliable evidence and approval to use it.
8. **Respect agency and privacy.** Support is invited, not demanded. Never imply that public sharing is required, morally superior, or appropriate for every goal.
9. **One post, one primary CTA.** Ask the audience to reflect, comment, save, share, visit, or create a goal—never all of them at once.
10. **Use the real brand voice.** Warm, grounded, clear, encouraging, and non-patronising. Avoid productivity-shame, "grind" language, salvation narratives, and guaranteed transformations.

## The core story formula

Build the copy in this order. Use only the parts needed for the requested length.

1. **The hole:** What is difficult about the hero's situation? Make it concrete and emotionally true.
2. **The turning point:** What small choice, insight, or act of support changes the direction?
3. **The guide and plan:** How does GoMotivateMe make the next step simpler? Mention the relevant capability in plain language.
4. **The possibility:** What more hopeful experience becomes available if the hero continues? Describe a feeling or process, not a promised result.
5. **The CTA:** Invite one proportionate action.

### Short form

`Recognisable tension → small truth → helpful next step → one CTA`

Example structure:

> It is hard to keep showing up when nobody can see the effort. A goal does not need a crowd—just a few people who know it matters. Create one shareable page, invite your people, and let every update be seen. **Who would you want in your corner?**

### Origin-of-empathy form (founder / company story)

`We saw or experienced [specific problem] → it mattered because [human consequence] → we built [specific, modest help] → so people can [supported possibility].`

This is the only useful version of the company origin story. Omit irrelevant founder biography, internal milestones, and generic ambition.

## Channel adaptations

### X / Twitter

- Open with one sharp sentence or a vivid observation.
- Use short lines and no more than one idea per post.
- Prefer a real progress moment, founder observation, or useful prompt over feature promotion.
- If linking to a public goal, explain why the moment matters before adding the link.
- End with a natural question or a single action.

### LinkedIn

- Begin with a human professional tension, not "Excited to announce."
- Use a short story or lesson with whitespace for scanning.
- Connect the founder/company perspective back to how it helps someone make progress.
- Keep the tone calm and credible; avoid grand claims and engagement bait.

### Instagram / TikTok / Reels

- Build the first 1–2 seconds around the emotional tension or the visual change—not the logo.
- Show a person, a goal moment, an update, or a supportive interaction.
- Use on-screen copy to name the hero's experience in plain language.
- Let the caption add context rather than repeating the video exactly.
- Use a small next step such as "Share this with someone whose goal you believe in."

### Email

- Make the subject and opening line about a relevant member need or opportunity.
- Use a compact narrative: moment → value → one main action.
- Distinguish marketing from account, safety, and accountability mail. Honor consent and the established marketing lifecycle.

### Website / launch copy

- The headline should name the audience benefit, not the product category.
- Explain the plan in three or fewer steps.
- Use product details as proof that the guide can help, not as the story itself.
- Pair the promise with an accurate visual from the approved brand and journey assets.

## Before-and-after examples

### Feature-first (avoid)

> We launched a new way to create public goal pages with reactions, milestones, and progress updates. Try GoMotivateMe today!

### Story-first (use)

> Some goals lose momentum in the quiet middle—after the excitement, before the finish line. A small check-in can change that. Share your progress with the people who want to see you keep going.

### Founder-as-hero (avoid)

> I built GoMotivateMe because I have always wanted to revolutionise motivation and grow a meaningful company.

### Guide origin story (use)

> We kept seeing people set goals they cared about, then carry the hard middle alone. GoMotivateMe exists to make it easier to invite a few people into the journey—so progress has somewhere to land.

### Overpromising (avoid)

> Public accountability will make you achieve your goal faster.

### Honest possibility (use)

> The right encouragement can make it easier to return to a goal when motivation dips.

## LLM workflow

Before drafting, gather or infer only these inputs:

1. Channel and format.
2. Audience and their specific moment of tension.
3. One real product capability or proof point to mention.
4. Desired emotional response.
5. One CTA.
6. Any approved facts, customer quotes, metrics, links, or assets.

If a required fact, outcome claim, customer story, or permission is missing, use a modest generic example or ask for the missing material. Do not invent testimonials, customer names, usage data, results, endorsements, or research.

## Copy-generation prompt

Copy and complete this prompt when using an LLM:

```text
You are writing [FORMAT] for GoMotivateMe, a public motivation app that helps people create a goal page, share it with chosen supporters, post progress, and receive encouragement.

Audience: [WHO THEY ARE]
Their specific moment or tension: [THE HOLE]
Relevant capability/proof: [ONE TRUE DETAIL]
Desired feeling: [FEELING]
Primary CTA: [ONE ACTION]
Approved assets or links: [ASSETS/LINKS]
Constraints: [LENGTH, TONE, PLATFORM RULES, REQUIRED FACTS]

Write with a story-first structure: recognition of the audience's tension, a brief emotional truth, GoMotivateMe as the empathetic and capable guide, a simple next step, and one CTA.

The audience is the hero. GoMotivateMe is the guide. Do not make the company, founder, or feature launch the hero. Do not invent facts, quotes, results, customers, or research. Do not promise that a user will reach their goal. Keep the language warm, grounded, specific, and free of shame or hype.

Return:
1. One finished draft.
2. Three alternative hooks.
3. A one-sentence explanation of which audience tension the draft addresses.
4. A factual-risk check listing any claim that needs verification before publishing.
```

## Final review checklist

- Is a real person pursuing a goal clearly the hero?
- Does the opening earn attention through a recognisable moment, rather than a company announcement?
- Does the copy show empathy before product detail?
- Is GoMotivateMe framed as a capable guide with a simple plan?
- Is the outcome hopeful but not guaranteed?
- Are all facts, quotes, statistics, and user claims verified and approved?
- Is there exactly one primary CTA?
- Does the copy sound like encouragement, not pressure?
- Does the image support the same story moment and follow the illustration guide?
